# behavioral interviews STAR and authentic experience narratives — Reference: Common experience prompts and the competencies they probe

# Scope and version

This lesson explains the supplied prompt families as common interview topics, not as a hidden company scoring rubric. The same question can probe several capabilities and employers vary in wording. The names below are the complete assigned group.

# How this group fits the mental model

Recognize the experience being requested, choose a truthful example, and make the relevant behavior visible. Do not force a story into a competency it does not demonstrate. Microsoft advises candidates to prepare specific examples, including how they applied feedback, worked with others, and adapted their approach. [Microsoft interview tips](https://careers.microsoft.com/v2/global/en/hiring-tips/interview-tips.html)

# Supplied names explained

- `Behavioral interview question` — A prompt asking for evidence from something that happened in the candidate’s experience. Answer with a real example and make the candidate’s role clear.
- `Situational interview question` — A hypothetical or role-related scenario asking how the candidate would respond. State assumptions, clarify constraints, outline actions and communication, and explain how success or risk would be evaluated.
- `Conflict with a teammate` — An experience prompt about a disagreement or interpersonal tension. Show listening, separating facts from positions, finding shared goals, and reaching a respectful resolution; do not portray the other person as a caricature.
- `Disagreement about a technical decision` — A prompt about competing designs or technical judgments. Explain requirements, evidence, alternatives, tradeoffs, decision ownership, and how you supported the final decision.
- `Mistake or failure` — A prompt about an unsuccessful action or outcome. Choose a real, bounded example; own your part, explain contributing conditions, repair what you could, and show changed behavior.
- `Learning from a failed approach` — A prompt emphasizing adaptation. Describe what evidence contradicted the approach, how you changed course, and how the learning influenced later work.
- `Ambiguous requirements` — A prompt about incomplete or conflicting needs. Explain how you identified stakeholders, clarified assumptions, made uncertainty visible, and validated a small interpretation before committing broadly.
- `Changing priorities` — A prompt about reprioritization. Show how you assessed impact and dependencies, communicated what moved and why, and managed work already in progress.
- `Tight deadline` — A prompt about time pressure. Explain scope tradeoffs, risk controls, communication, and what you explicitly deferred; do not celebrate unsafe shortcuts as heroism.
- `Production incident contribution` — A prompt about responding to service impact. Distinguish observation from hypothesis, describe your role and safe actions, verify user recovery, and explain learning afterward. Avoid revealing sensitive operational details.
- `Taking ownership` — A prompt about recognizing and following through on a responsibility, including surfacing problems and coordinating a resolution. Ownership is not taking sole credit or silently absorbing impossible work.
- `Working across teams` — A prompt about coordinating distinct groups. Explain interfaces, shared outcomes, decision rights, communication, and how you handled dependency or vocabulary differences.
- `Influencing without authority` — A prompt about changing a decision or behavior without direct control. Use evidence, understand others’ constraints, offer a small experiment, and accept legitimate decision ownership.
- `Receiving difficult feedback` — A prompt about feedback that was uncomfortable or corrective. Describe how you listened, clarified, evaluated it, acted, and later assessed whether your behavior changed.
- `Giving constructive feedback` — A prompt about raising a concern usefully. Describe a specific observation and impact, how you invited discussion, and the outcome; avoid claiming that bluntness is the same as honesty.
- `Mentoring a teammate` — A prompt about enabling another person’s growth. Show how you understood their goals, adapted support, preserved their ownership, and observed progress without claiming credit for their success.
- `Helping an underperforming teammate` — A prompt about supporting someone struggling. Respect privacy, clarify expectations and blockers, offer specific help, and involve a manager or formal process only within your role and policy.
- `Improving a process` — A prompt about changing recurring work. Identify the friction, involve affected people, try a proportionate change, and show evidence of improvement or an honest limitation.
- `Delivering measurable impact` — A prompt about an outcome. Explain the metric, baseline, period, population, and your contribution; if it was not measured, provide qualitative evidence instead.
- `Ethical judgment` — A prompt about a values or safety conflict. Identify affected parties and principles, raise concerns through an appropriate channel, protect people or data, and explain the decision without disclosing protected details.
- `Customer or user focus` — A prompt about understanding user needs and consequences. Show how you gathered input, recognized impact, and adjusted decisions; do not assume internal convenience equals user value.
- `Handling competing priorities` — A prompt about multiple important demands. Explain criteria, dependencies, stakeholder alignment, explicit deferrals, and how you revisited priorities when conditions changed.
- `Making a decision with incomplete information` — A prompt about uncertainty. State what was known, the cost of delay, reversible options, a test or checkpoint, and what evidence would change the decision.
- `Adapting to a new technology` — A prompt about learning. Describe the concrete task, learning strategy, validation, limitations, and how you transferred knowledge; do not equate tool use with mastery.
- `Explaining a complex idea` — A prompt about communication. Explain how you adapted detail and language to the audience, checked understanding, and used feedback to refine the explanation.

# Cross-item comparison

| Prompt family distinction | Emphasis |
| --- | --- |
| Behavioral and situational | Past evidence versus a hypothetical plan. Label experience accurately. |
| Conflict and technical disagreement | Relationship/process repair versus resolution of competing technical evidence; either may involve both. |
| Failure and learning from a failed approach | Failure focuses on accountable response and outcome; learning emphasizes how evidence changed subsequent behavior. |
| Taking ownership and working across teams | Personal follow-through versus coordination across responsibility boundaries. |
| Tight deadline and competing priorities | A time constraint versus choosing among multiple demands; both require explicit tradeoffs and communication. |
| Measurable impact and user focus | Evidence about an outcome versus grounding choices in user needs. A metric alone does not prove user value. |

# Examples

### Select evidence for “influencing without authority”

Choose a real situation where you had no decision power, understood another team’s constraint, presented evidence or a low-cost experiment, and respected the final decision. An example that merely says “I convinced them because I was right” does not reveal listening or collaboration.

### Answer an ethical judgment prompt

If retrospective, describe a real concern, the people/data at risk, the safe channel used, and the outcome you can disclose. If hypothetical, ask about urgency and policy constraints, contain immediate harm, preserve evidence, and escalate to the appropriate owner. Do not claim to have legal authority you do not possess.

# Common mistakes

- Choosing a dramatic story that does not answer the competency asked about.
- Describing a conflict by blaming or mocking the other person.
- Presenting overtime and skipped controls as the only way to meet a tight deadline.
- Saying “I communicated” without explaining audience, content, timing, and decision.
- Claiming that a team-wide metric was caused entirely by one personal action.
- Treating a hypothetical as a past event or a personal project as paid professional experience.
- Revealing employer, customer, security, or personal details that are not appropriate to disclose.
- Assuming every behavioral answer must end in a clear success; a thoughtful bounded failure can show stronger learning.

# Knowledge checks with answers

1. **What changes between behavioral and situational answers?** A behavioral answer uses a true past example; a situational answer states assumptions and gives a plan.
2. **How can a candidate discuss conflict constructively?** Explain shared goals, listening, evidence, actions, and resolution without caricaturing the other person.
3. **What belongs in a tight-deadline story?** Scope decisions, explicit risks/deferred work, communication, and safe validation—not just hours worked.
4. **How do you support an impact claim?** Give metric definition, baseline, scope and source, or use specific qualitative evidence if no reliable metric exists.
5. **What does influencing without authority require?** Understand constraints, build a reasoned case or experiment, communicate, and respect legitimate decision rights.
